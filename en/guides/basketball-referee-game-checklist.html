The basketball referee's pre-game checklist

A well-refereed game often starts the day before. Here is the list to follow, from packing your bag to getting home, so you arrive calm and forget nothing.

The day before: re-read your appointment

Open your appointment and check four things: the game time, the exact venue address (some clubs play in several gyms), the level of the game and the name of your partner or partners. An appointment can change after you receive it: earlier tip-off, different gym, postponed game. It's the most common mistake and the easiest to avoid.

Take the chance to contact your partner. A short message is enough: planned arrival time, whether you can travel together, which shirt colour to wear if you need to match.

The referee's kit bag

  • Full kit: shirt, trousers, belt, socks, clean shoes. Take a spare shirt for long days.
  • Two whistles, one as a backup, with their lanyard or finger grip.
  • Watch or stopwatch for time-outs and warm-up.
  • ID and licence, in case you're asked for them.
  • Pen and something to write on: technical fouls, incidents, notes for your report.
  • Water bottle, a snack and a towel.
  • For a tournament: spare clothes and food for between games.

The easiest way: keep a bag always ready, and restock it after each game rather than before the next one.

Travel

Work out your departure time from the time you're expected at the venue, not from tip-off. Add a margin for traffic, parking and finding the right entrance. Write down the mileage: it's used to calculate your mileage allowance and the real cost of the trip.

Arriving at the venue

  • Introduce yourself to the venue manager or the home club's representative.
  • Find the changing room, the scorer's table and the table officials.
  • Check the court, the baskets, the clocks and the foul display.
  • Check the scoresheet before tip-off: teams, players, coaches.

Pre-game talk with your partner

Ten minutes is enough to agree: who does what at the start, positioning mechanics, substitutions and time-outs, and how you'll communicate during the game. Talk about the context too: a derby, a key game in the standings, a team known for arguing calls. Being aligned before you walk on court prevents most tricky situations.

Warm-up

Refereeing means running, stopping, starting again. A ten to fifteen minute warm-up (light jog, ankle and hip mobility, a few sprints) reduces the risk of injury and gets you in rhythm from the first play.

After the game

  • Sign the scoresheet after reading it through.
  • Write down any incidents to report and the plays you want to review.
  • Have a two-minute debrief with your partner: one thing that went well, one thing to work on.
  • Mark the game as refereed and check the expected match fee, so you can track its payment.
